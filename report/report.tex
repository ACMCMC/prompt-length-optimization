\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

% Idea: we're doing prompt optimization for LMs, this has applications in e.g. security research (find jailbreaks) or improving performance (e.g. improving prompts to maximize their effectiveness on downstream tasks)
Language models (LMs) have grown in popularity due to their applicability on a range of NLP tasks. However, enabling models to follow specific commands effectively remains a challenge, particularly given the (often) non-deterministic behavior of instruction-following LMs. Prompt optimization is an area of research that proposes an avenue to deal with such difficulty, aiming to find a set of tokens that is likely to elicit a specific response from the model. This has applications in several fields, including security research (e.g., finding jailbreaks), performance enhancement or task-specific tuning.

% Idea: highlight gap between likelihood-only search and static pruning
Despite the amount of interest built around prompt optimization, some challenges remain. In this paper, we focus on one overlooked aspect of prompt optimization: many search methods focus on exclusively optimizing for likelihood, while ignoring length. However, shorter prompts are often preferred in practice, as they reduce inference costs and latency and are easier for humans to utilize. Therefore, our work seeks to balance the two objectives at the same time: maximizing the likelihood of a target completion while minimizing prompt length.

% Idea: explain our external policy and scope (plug-in optimizer, no retrieval/safety)
To that end, we train a small external policy, independent of the objective LM (which we treat as a black box). The policy decides when to shrink, keep, or grow a prompt, while any plug-in optimizer adjusts tokens. We do not design a new optimizer and accept the limits of whichever one is used. We also avoid retrieval and safety objectives; we only balance likelihood and length.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

% Idea: prompt optimization is popular, many methods exist
Prompt optimization is a very active area of research, with many authors proposing a variety of techniques. Therefore, we group prior work into four areas:

% Idea: gradient-based discrete prompt optimization
\paragraph{Discrete optimization.} These methods keep prompts in the token space and use gradients to move among words. HotFlip-style edits and \citet{shin2020autoprompt} pick candidate tokens with gradient signals. \citet{wen2023hard} smooth the search with continuous helpers and then project back to tokens. Adversarial suffix work like \citet{zou2023universal} and faster probe sampling \citep{zhao2024accelerating} show that gradient-guided discrete search can find strong suffixes when we have white-box access.

% Idea: continuous prompt optimization and projection
\paragraph{Continuous optimization.} Soft prompt tuning swaps hard tokens for learned embedding vectors that we train with backpropagation \citep{lester2021power}. Later work looks at safety and robustness in the embedding space \citep{schwinn2024soft}. More recently, \citet{wang2025dpro} study direct prompt optimization with continuous representations, using stronger goals to narrow the gap between soft prompts and discrete use.

% Idea: prompt compression for efficiency
\paragraph{Prompt compression.} To cut inference cost, coarse-to-fine pruning methods compress demonstrations and questions with perplexity signals from a smaller LM, as in LLMLingua \citep{jiang2023llmlingua} and its distilled follow-up \citep{pan2024llmlingua2}. Gist tokens \citep{mu2023gist} learn short continuous summaries; context-aware compression uses sentence encodings \citep{liskavets2024prompt}. TACO-RL \citep{shandilya2024taco} adds task-aware rewards to compression to keep outputs accurate under fixed length budgets.

% Idea: reinforcement learning for prompt editing and safety
\paragraph{Reinforcement learning.} RL has been used to optimize prompts or policies that pick edits over sequences \citep{ramamurthy2022rlnlp}, and to align or adversarially steer LMs with discrete action spaces. Our work links to this line by viewing length control as a policy that decides when to compress versus improve likelihood, not only by tuning embeddings.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\begin{figure*}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/gcg_length_sweep.pdf}
    \label{fig:gcg_length_sweep}
    \caption{\textbf{GCG optimizes for likelihood, not for length.} Blah blah blah}
\end{figure*}

We seek a suffix \(s\) that makes a frozen LM \(p_\theta\) assign high probability to a fixed target completion while keeping \(s\) as short as possible. Let \(x\) denote the fixed prefix (e.g., a harmful user query) and \(y\) the fixed target completion (e.g., the corresponding harmful response). The optimization problem is
\begin{equation}
  \min_{s} \mathcal{L}(s) = -\log p(y \mid x, s),
\end{equation}
with an additional preference for small \(|s|\). The model parameters are not updated; all edits act on \(s\). Each edit either changes the suffix length (adding or removing a token) or replaces suffix tokens in place.

We formulate this as a finite-horizon Markov decision process (MDP). A state \(z\) encapsulates the current suffix length and likelihood statistics (e.g., log-likelihood and length ratios). Actions operate exclusively on the suffix—either invoking content optimization or performing length edits—and the reward balances two objectives: increasing the likelihood of \(y\) and reducing the suffix length.




\subsection{State Space}
\label{subsec:state}

We maintain a compact, five-dimensional state per rollout that summarizes suffix size, model fit, and episode progress:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=0pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item normalized length \(|s|/|s_0|\), where \(|s_0|\) is the initial suffix length at the start of the episode;
  \item current length \(|s|\);
  \item current log-likelihood \(\ell = \log p_\theta(y \mid x_{\text{pref}}, s)\);
  \item likelihood ratio \(\ell / (\ell_0+\varepsilon)\), where \(\ell_0\) is the log-likelihood at the episode start and \(\varepsilon\) is a small positive constant for numerical stability;
  \item episode progress \(t/T\), with \(t\) the current step and \(T\) the episode length.
\end{enumerate}
These statistics are inexpensive to compute and keep the policy lightweight.


\subsection{Action Space}
\label{subsec:actions}

Actions operate solely on the suffix \(s\): \(\{\text{optimize}, \text{shrink}, \text{grow}\}\). \emph{Optimize} runs the inner optimizer (Section~\ref{subsec:modes}) on the current suffix content without changing its length. \emph{Shrink} removes the last active suffix token (if \(|s|>0\)). \emph{Grow} adds one suffix token up to \(L_{\max}\); the new slot is initialized by the optimizer (a random non-special token in discrete mode, a BOS embedding in continuous mode). The prefix and target completion remain fixed throughout.

\subsection{Reward Function}
\label{subsec:reward}

At the end of each episode we assign a terminal reward as follows
\begin{equation}
  r \;=\; \alpha\,\tilde{\ell} \;-\; \beta\,\frac{|s|}{L_{\max}},
\end{equation}
where \(\tilde{\ell}\) denotes the per-token log-likelihood of the target completion \(y\) under the final suffix, \(\alpha>0\) weights accuracy, and \(\beta>0\) penalizes length. This terminal reward is propagated backward through the episode via a discount factor \(\gamma\), so every action contributing to the final suffix receives credit in proportion to its recency. Formally, for an episode of length \(T\), the return at step \(t\) is
\begin{equation}
  R_t \;=\; \gamma^{\,T-1-t} \, r, \quad t = 0,\dots,T-1.
\end{equation}
These discounted returns are then centered per prompt group to form advantages for the GRPO update. Step-level rewards computed during the rollout are used for monitoring only; the policy update uses the terminal signal exclusively.




\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

% Idea: describe pipeline from inputs to policy and inner optimizers
We pair a frozen LM with a small policy network. The policy decides when to shrink, grow, or call an inner optimizer; the inner optimizer improves the suffix tokens or embeddings. We train the policy with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) while leaving the LM untouched.

\subsection{Policy Network Architecture}
\label{subsec:policy}

% Idea: lightweight MLP with epsilon-greedy sampling
The policy is a two-layer MLP that maps the five state features to three action logits. We apply a temperature, softmax, and epsilon-greedy mixing to sample actions. We include an entropy bonus and clip ratios as in GRPO to keep updates stable. Exploration rate $\epsilon$ decays each episode; gradients only update the policy parameters.

\subsection{Optimization Modes}
\label{subsec:modes}

% Idea: plug-in inner optimizers for different search spaces
We support three plug-in inner optimizers; the policy is agnostic to which one is used.

\subsubsection{Continuous Optimization}
\label{subsubsec:continuous}

% Idea: optimize suffix embeddings directly
In continuous mode we work with suffix embeddings instead of tokens. We keep a trainable block of shape $[B, L_{\max}, D]$ where active slots start with the BOS embedding. We run Adam to increase the log-likelihood of the target completion; prefix and completion embeddings stay frozen. When we grow the suffix, we activate one more slot (it gets initialized later), and when we shrink it we turn off the last slot. This happens through the attention mask. At the end we project active embeddings to their nearest vocabulary tokens using L2 distance for reporting.

\subsubsection{Continuous with Projection}
\label{subsubsec:continuous_proj}

% Idea: add distance regularization to stay near the embedding table
Continuous\_proj extends the continuous approach by pushing suffix embeddings to stay near real tokens during training. For each active position we measure the distance to the vocabulary and add it as a penalty in the loss. The full objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = -\log p(y\mid x,s) + w_{\text{proj}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}},
  \label{eq:continuous_proj_loss}
\end{equation}
where $w_{\text{proj}}$ is the projection weight and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{proj}}$ is the mean distance from active suffix embeddings to their nearest vocabulary tokens. We use L2 distance in our experiments. At evaluation we project suffix embeddings to tokens before computing the reward; this makes sure the policy sees scores that match discrete prompts. We also track the average projection distance to see how easy it is to deploy the learned embeddings.

\subsubsection{Discrete Optimization}
\label{subsubsec:discrete}

In discrete mode we run a Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) update on the suffix tokens~\citep{zou2023universal}. At each GCG step: (i) compute gradients of the log-likelihood with respect to the current suffix token positions; (ii) for each position, form a small candidate pool by replacing that token with top-$k$ alternatives suggested by the gradient; (iii) batch-evaluate these one-token candidates; and (iv) keep the best-scoring suffix. Each GCG step therefore changes at most one suffix token. This yields a greedy, gradient-guided token search that improves log-likelihood in a few forward/backward passes while policy actions handle length changes.



\subsection{Training Procedure}
\label{subsec:training}

We run batched, fixed-horizon episodes with multiple rollouts per prompt. For each batch we build inputs, roll out \(T\) steps, and collect states, actions, and log-probs. After each episode, compute the terminal reward, discount it backward, center advantages per prompt (GRPO), and run several GRPO epochs with clipping and entropy bonus.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{subsec:implementation}

We construct a fixed batched layout with three segments per example: an immutable prefix, a trainable suffix, and an immutable completion. Prefixes are left-padded and completions right-padded with their own attention masks. The suffix is a trainable buffer of shape \([B, L_{\max}]\) (tokens in discrete mode, embeddings in continuous mode) with an attention mask marking active positions; the first \texttt{init\_len} slots are active at initialization. Length edits toggle this mask: \textit{grow} activates the next inactive slot, \textit{shrink} deactivates the last active slot. Newly activated slots are initialized by the optimizer when the suffix is updated.

For forward passes, the three segments are concatenated and a combined attention mask is built; suffix start indices are tracked so discrete optimizers can splice candidate suffixes efficiently. In continuous modes, concatenated embeddings are provided; in discrete mode, concatenated token IDs are used. Edits and gradients are confined to the suffix; prefix and completion remain fixed. All experiments use a frozen \texttt{Pythia-70M} backbone.

\section*{Limitations}

% Idea: acknowledge scope and potential issues
As with any work, we have our limitations, and for scientific rigor we want to be transparent about them. We only test on a small LM (\texttt{Pythia-70M}), so results might be different with larger models. The policy is trained for a fixed maximum suffix length, which makes it less flexible. We also assume white-box access to the LM for gradient computation; this is not always possible in practice. Our experiments focus on adversarial prompt discovery instead of general prompt optimization tasks. Finally, the policy might not work well across different domains or model families without retraining.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

% Idea: discuss dual-use and responsible research
This work focuses on prompt optimization techniques that can be used for both good and bad purposes. We frame our experiments using adversarial suffix generation (a common setting in the literature), but we know that these methods could be misused to create harmful content. Our goal is to better understand prompt optimization and length trade-offs, not to help malicious uses. Researchers who use these techniques should think about the ethical implications and add proper safeguards. We release our code expecting it will be used responsibly for research that helps the community.
